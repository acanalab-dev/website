How to split costs for a shared car

A shared car is one of the bigger costs couples have, and one of the messiest to split. Here's how to list every cost, choose an equal, by-use or mixed split, and handle repairs, fines and selling, with a worked example.

A shared car is one of the bigger costs many couples have after rent, and one of the messiest to split. There's the loan or purchase price, insurance, fuel, parking, servicing and the surprise repair. Usually one of you also drives it more than the other. This guide walks through listing the costs, choosing a split (equal, by use, or a mix of both), handling repairs and fines, and agreeing what happens if you sell it, with a worked example.

Step 1: List every car cost

Car costs fall into three groups. Write them all down, using a monthly figure for each:

  • Fixed costs: loan or lease payments, insurance, road tax or registration, a parking permit. You pay these whether you drive or not.
  • Running costs: fuel or charging, tolls, parking, car washes. These go up the more you drive.
  • Upkeep: servicing, tyres, inspections and repairs. Add up a typical year and divide by 12.
Tip: check last year's statements for car costs. Servicing, tyres and inspections are easy to forget because they only come once or twice a year.

Step 2: Be clear about ownership

Before you split anything, write down the basics:

  • Whose name is the car in, and whose name is on any loan or lease. That person is usually the one the lender or authorities deal with.
  • Who paid for it, including the deposit or purchase price, and how much each of you put in.
  • Whose car it is in practice. A car one of you owned before and still mostly drives is different from one you bought together for both of you.

These points matter most if you ever sell the car or go separate ways. Rules on ownership and loans differ between countries, so for specific questions talk to a qualified professional.

Step 3: Choose a split

Equal (50/50)

Every car cost split in half. Simple, and it works well when you both use the car about the same amount.

By use

Each of you pays in line with how much you drive. Fair when one of you drives much more, for example for a daily commute. It needs a rough record of kilometres.

A mix: fixed costs equal, running costs by use

Many couples land here. You split loan, insurance and upkeep equally because the car is there for both of you, and fuel by who drives more. It's fair without needing exact numbers for everything.

If your incomes differ a lot, you can also split the fixed part by income instead of 50/50. Our proportional splitting guide shows how.

Step 4: A worked example

Tea and Luka share one car. Tea drives it to work every day; Luka uses it mostly at weekends. Their monthly costs:

CostPer monthType
Loan payment€200Fixed
Insurance€70Fixed
Road tax and registration (€360 a year)€30Fixed
Servicing and tyres (about €600 a year)€50Upkeep
Fuel€160Running
Total€510

They drive about 1,600 km a month: Tea 1,200 km (75%) and Luka 400 km (25%). Here's how three splits compare:

SplitTea paysLuka pays
50/50 for everything€255€255
Fixed and upkeep 50/50, fuel by use€295€215
Everything by use (75/25)€382.50€127.50

For the mixed split, the €350 of fixed costs and upkeep is €175 each. Fuel at 75/25 is €120 for Tea and €40 for Luka. That gives €295 and €215.

They choose the mix. The car is there for both of them, but Tea's commute uses most of the fuel. Every few months they check the kilometres again, and if Luka starts driving more, they adjust.

Another simple option is a cost per kilometre: €510 ÷ 1,600 km is about €0.32 per km. That can be handy if one of you owns the car and the other borrows it now and then.

Step 5: Repairs, fines and other surprises

  • Normal repairs and wear: split them like your other fixed costs. A big repair is a good use for a shared buffer, see our couples emergency fund guide.
  • Damage from one person's accident: agree in advance. Many couples split it anyway, since accidents happen; others have the driver pay the insurance excess.
  • Parking and speeding fines: usually the driver pays.
  • Personal trips: a long solo trip to see your own family or friends can count as personal fuel, if you both agree.

Step 6: Agree what happens if you sell it

Decide early how you'd split the money from a sale. Common choices are in proportion to what each of you paid towards the purchase, or equally if you bought it together 50/50. Write it down in a sentence or two. It's a calm conversation now and a hard one later.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Only splitting fuel. Insurance, tax and servicing are often more than the fuel.
  • Forgetting the once-a-year costs. Turn them into a monthly figure.
  • No record of who paid for the car. It matters most if you sell it or separate.
  • Never revisiting the split. A new job or commute can change who drives most.

Frequently asked questions

How should couples split car costs?

Many split fixed costs like the loan, insurance and servicing 50/50 and fuel by who drives more. Others split everything equally or everything by use.

How do we split fuel if one of us drives more?

Note roughly how many kilometres each of you drives in a month and split fuel in the same ratio, for example 75/25.

Who pays for parking or speeding fines?

Usually the driver who got the fine pays it.

What happens to the car money if we sell it?

Agree in advance, for example in proportion to what each of you paid towards the purchase, or equally if you bought it together 50/50.
