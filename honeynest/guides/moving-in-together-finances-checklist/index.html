Moving in together: a finances checklist for couples

Moving in together goes smoother when you've talked about money first. This checklist covers the lease and deposit, the money conversation, which bills to split, and how to handle your first month.

Moving in together changes your money as much as your address. Talking it through before the move is much easier than sorting it out after the first surprise bill. Use this checklist to cover the essentials, from the deposit to who pays the internet, in one or two calm conversations.

Before you sign the lease

  • Agree on a budget for rent or mortgage. Work from what you can both comfortably afford, not the most the landlord or bank will accept.
  • Whose name goes on the lease? If only one of you signs, that person is usually the one responsible to the landlord. Talk about what happens if you break up or one of you wants to move out.
  • The deposit. Decide who pays it and how it's split. Write it down, including how it comes back to each of you when you move out.
  • Moving costs. Movers, a van, cleaning, new keys. Agree on a rough total and how you'll split it.
  • Furniture and big purchases. Agree on who owns what you buy together, and what happens to it if you split up. A short note in a shared document is enough.

The money conversation

This is the part people skip, and it's the most useful. You don't need to share everything at once, but these are worth an honest answer:

  • Income. Roughly what each of you takes home each month. You need this if you plan to split bills by income.
  • Debts and commitments. Student loans, car payments, credit cards, money you send to family. They affect what each of you can put toward shared costs.
  • Savings goals. A trip, a car, a home, a cushion for emergencies. Shared goals and personal goals are both fine; say which is which.
  • Spending styles. One of you may be a saver and the other a spender. Neither is wrong, but it helps to name it.
  • What “fair” means to each of you. Equal amounts, equal percentages, or something else.
Tip: have this talk somewhere relaxed, with the numbers in front of you, and agree to revisit it in three months. It takes the pressure off getting everything right the first time.

Joint, separate, or both?

There are three common setups:

  • Fully separate. Each pays their part of the bills from their own account, and you settle the difference regularly.
  • Yours, mine and ours. Each keeps a personal account and you both pay a set amount into a joint account for shared costs. Many couples start here.
  • Fully joint. All income goes into one place. Usually a later step, once you're both comfortable.

Whichever you choose, keep a record of shared spending so you both see the same numbers.

Your first month

  • Set up the bills. Decide who registers each utility and which account or card it's paid from.
  • Record recurring costs once. Rent, internet and subscriptions repeat every month, so set them up as recurring in whatever you use to track.
  • Log the extras. The first month always has surprises: a missing lamp, cleaning supplies, a welcome dinner. Note who paid.
  • Agree on a check-in date. For example, the first weekend of each month. Look at what each of you paid and settle the difference.
  • Plan a small buffer. New homes come with unexpected costs. Agree on how you'll handle them before they arrive.

If things change

Jobs change, incomes change and plans change. Agree now on a few “what ifs” so nobody has to raise them at a hard moment:

  • What happens if one of you loses income for a while?
  • How will you split costs if one of you starts working from home and uses more heating or electricity?
  • If you separate, who keeps the flat, and how are the deposit and shared furniture handled?

None of this is unromantic. It's the boring groundwork that lets you enjoy the new place without money tension in the background.

Frequently asked questions

What should couples discuss about money before moving in?

Income, debts and regular commitments, savings goals, spending styles, which costs you'll share, and how you'll split them. It also helps to agree how the deposit and shared furniture are handled if things change.

What bills should couples split when living together?

Usually rent or mortgage, utilities, internet, groceries and household supplies. Personal costs like your own phone plan, clothes and hobbies usually stay with each person.

Should we open a joint account when we move in?

It's optional. Many couples start with a joint account just for shared costs and keep personal accounts, while others pay from separate accounts and settle the difference monthly.

How do we split rent if one of us earns more?

Some couples split rent 50/50, others in proportion to income. For example, with take-home pay of 3,000 and 2,000, a proportional split is 60/40.
