A fair way to split household chores and costs

Fairness at home is rarely only about money or only about housework. Here's how to put chores and costs on one page, agree what fair means for you, and check it once a month, with a worked example.

When couples argue about fairness at home, it's rarely only about money or only about housework. It's usually both: one of you pays more of the bills, the other does more of the cooking, and nobody is quite sure whether it evens out. This guide walks through a simple way to see all the work and all the costs on one page, agree what fair means for the two of you, and check it once a month, with a worked example.

Step 1: List every chore, with rough time

Most couples underestimate how much housework there is until they write it down. Sit down together and list everything that keeps the home running, with a rough number of minutes per week:

ChoreTime per weekWho does it now
Cooking dinners4 hoursNina
Grocery shopping1.5 hoursNina
Dishes and kitchen2 hoursPetar
Cleaning and vacuuming2 hoursNina
Laundry1.5 hoursNina
Bins, recycling and small repairs1 hourPetar
Bills, bookings and paperwork2 hoursPetar

The times don't need to be exact. A rough estimate you both accept is enough to start a fair conversation.

Step 2: Count the invisible work too

Some of the work at home doesn't look like a chore. It's the planning and remembering: noticing the milk is running out, booking the plumber, remembering birthdays, renewing the insurance, keeping track of who paid for what. It takes time and attention, and it's easy for one person to carry it without anyone noticing.

Add these to your list as their own lines, for example “meal planning”, “appointments and bookings” or “tracking shared expenses”. Naming them is often the most useful part of the whole exercise.

Tip: for one week, both of you jot down every household task as you do it, including the small ones. It settles “I do everything” debates with facts instead of feelings.

Step 3: Agree what fair means for you

Fair doesn't have to mean equal. Common ways couples think about it:

  • Equal time: you each spend about the same hours on housework.
  • Equal total effort: paid work and housework together. If one of you works much longer hours, the other may do more at home, if you both agree.
  • By preference: each does the chores they mind least, and you share the ones you both dislike.

Whichever you choose, say it out loud and agree on it. Most resentment comes from two different, unspoken ideas of fair. If the conversation feels tense, our guide on how to talk about money with your partner has tips that work for housework too.

Step 4: Divide the chores

With the list and your idea of fair agreed, divide it up:

  1. Start with preferences. If one of you enjoys cooking and the other doesn't mind dishes, that's an easy pair.
  2. Share or rotate the unpopular jobs. Bathrooms, ironing or bins can switch every week or month.
  3. Give each chore a clear owner. “Whoever notices” usually means one person always notices first.
  4. Own the whole task. Doing the laundry includes noticing it's needed, washing, drying and putting it away.

Step 5: A worked example with chores and costs

Nina and Petar split their €2,000 a month of shared costs 50/50, so €1,000 each. From the table in Step 1, Nina does 9 hours of chores a week and Petar does 5. That's 14 hours together, so an even split would be 7 hours each. They both work full time and agree that equal time feels fair to them.

They look at three options:

OptionChores per weekMonthly costs
Rebalance chores: Petar takes over laundry and half the grocery shoppingNina 6.75 h, Petar 7.25 h€1,000 each
Pay for help: a cleaner for 2 hours a week at €80 a month, added to shared costsNina 7 h, Petar 5 h€1,040 each
Combine: cleaner, and Petar takes over half the grocery shoppingNina 6.25 h, Petar 5.75 h€1,040 each

In the first option, laundry (1.5 hours) and half the shopping (0.75 hours) move to Petar: Nina goes from 9 to 6.75 hours and Petar from 5 to 7.25 hours. In the second, the cleaner takes over Nina's 2 hours of cleaning, and the €80 cost is split 50/50, adding €40 each to €1,000. The third combines both.

They choose the third option. It costs them €40 more each per month, but they both get time back and the split feels even. None of these options is the right answer for everyone. What matters is that they chose together, with the numbers in front of them.

Some couples also agree to adjust the money split when the chore split stays uneven on purpose, for example when one person has a much longer commute. That's fine if you both agree openly. The proportional splitting guide shows how to work out a different split.

Step 6: Keep household spending simple

Chores and money meet in the shopping basket. Whoever does the grocery run or buys cleaning supplies often pays at the till, and over a month that adds up. A few habits keep it fair:

  • Log each shared household purchase and note who paid.
  • Treat regular costs like a cleaner as a recurring shared cost, not a favour one of you pays for.
  • Settle up once a month instead of after every shop.

Our guide on splitting groceries with your partner covers the shopping side in more detail.

Step 7: Check in once a month

A setup that's fair in January may feel unfair by March, especially if work hours change or someone is ill. Add two quick questions to your monthly money check-in:

  1. Does the chore split still feel fair to both of us?
  2. Did anything new come up that needs an owner?

Then settle any shared costs for the month. The guide on how to settle up who owes what walks through the math.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Only counting visible chores. Planning and admin take time too.
  • Keeping score every day. Look at the month, not each evening.
  • “Helping” instead of owning. A chore with no owner falls back on whoever cares most.
  • Never revisiting the split. Lives change, and the split should too.

Frequently asked questions

What is a fair way to split household chores?

List every chore with rough weekly time, including planning and admin, agree what fair means for you (equal time, equal total effort or preference), and give each chore a clear owner.

Should the partner who does more chores pay less?

Some couples agree that, especially when one does much more housework on purpose. It's optional and works when you both agree openly.

Is paying for a cleaner a shared cost?

If it saves both of you time on shared housework, many couples treat it as a shared cost and split it like rent or bills.

How often should we review the chore split?

Once a month is enough. Add it to your monthly money check-in and adjust when work hours or routines change.
