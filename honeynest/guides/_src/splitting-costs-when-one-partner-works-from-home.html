When one of you works from home and the other goes to an office, the home quietly gets more expensive. The heating runs all day, the lights and laptop are on, the internet may need an upgrade, and a spare room may turn into an office. It's easy for the office-going partner to feel they're paying for someone else's workplace, or for the home worker to feel they're saving the household a commute. This guide walks through estimating the extra costs, choosing a fair way to handle them, and checking the split as the seasons change, with a worked example.

Step 1: List what changes when someone works from home

Not every cost goes up, and some go down. Make a short list of both:

  • Often higher: heating or cooling during the day, electricity for lights and equipment, a faster internet plan, coffee and lunches at home, office furniture or supplies.
  • Sometimes lower: the home worker's commute, lunches out and work clothes. These are usually personal savings, not shared ones.
  • Space: a room used as an office isn't free, especially if you'd otherwise rent a smaller home.

Separate shared household costs (energy, internet, rent) from personal ones (the home worker's own equipment and commute). Only the shared ones need a decision.

Step 2: Estimate the extra cost

You don't need to be exact. A rough figure you both accept is enough. Three simple ways to estimate it:

  1. Compare before and after. If one of you started working from home recently, compare the same months' bills from before and after.
  2. Compare work days and away days. Some energy providers show daily use in their app. Compare a home-office weekday with a weekday when nobody's home.
  3. Agree a fixed amount. If neither works, agree a reasonable monthly figure, for example a share of the winter heating bill, and adjust later.
Tip: estimate separately for winter and summer. Working from home often adds much more to heating bills in January than in June.

Step 3: Pick how to handle it

Keep everything 50/50

Many couples simply ignore the difference. It's small compared with rent, and the home worker often takes on more household tasks during the day, like deliveries, laundry or letting in a plumber. That's a perfectly fair choice if you both agree.

The home worker pays the extra

You split the household's “normal” costs as usual, and the partner working from home covers the estimated extra. It's the most precise option.

Adjust the overall split

Instead of tracking each bill, you shift the split slightly, for example from 50/50 to 52/48, to cover extra energy and the use of a room. It's simple once agreed.

If the home worker gets a work-from-home allowance or expense payments from their employer, it makes sense to use them towards the extra household costs. Rules on allowances and tax differ by country and employer, so for anything specific, ask the employer or a qualified professional.

Step 4: A worked example

Mila works from home four days a week; Bruno goes to the office. Before Mila started working from home, their energy bill averaged €100 a month and their internet was €25. Now energy averages €140 and they've upgraded to a €40 internet plan for Mila's video calls.

For the second option, the €125 they'd pay anyway is split 50/50, so €62.50 each, and Mila adds the €55 extra.

Mila's employer pays a €30 monthly home-office allowance. They agree Mila puts that towards the extra, so the extra she pays from her own pocket is €25. In the end they settle on something in between: Mila covers the €55 extra (using her allowance), and in return Bruno takes over more of the evening cooking, since Mila handles deliveries and laundry during the day.

They also look at rent. Their €1,200 flat has a small second room that's now Mila's office. They talk about splitting rent 52/48 (€624 and €576) but decide the energy arrangement is enough. Our guide on how to split rent with your partner covers rent splits in more detail.

Step 5: Check it when the seasons change

Energy costs swing between summer and winter, and work patterns change. Look at the arrangement:

  • at the start of the heating season and again in spring,
  • when either of you changes job or work pattern,
  • when you move to a new home.

A quick look during your monthly money check-in is usually enough. Our monthly money check-in template has a simple agenda.

Step 6: Don't forget the non-money side

Working from home changes more than the bills. The person at home often ends up doing more of the small daytime jobs, while the other might do more in the evenings. Talk about that openly too. Our guide to a fair way to split household chores and costs helps you see the whole picture.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Guessing without numbers. Compare bills before you decide.
  • Using winter bills all year. Estimate summer and winter separately.
  • Forgetting employer allowances. If there is one, decide how it's used.
  • Only counting money. Daytime chores and deliveries count too.
