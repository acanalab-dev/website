You've kept track of shared costs all month. Now comes the part that trips many couples up: turning a list of payments into one clear number for who owes whom. This guide gives you a simple formula, shows how to handle personal items and money you've already sent each other, and walks through a full example with real numbers.

Step 1: Gather the month's numbers

Before any math, make sure the month is complete. You need three things:

  • Every shared expense and who paid it: rent, bills, groceries, things you did together.
  • Any personal items one of you paid for the other, like a concert ticket or a gift for their family.
  • Any money you've already sent each other during the month.

If you haven't been keeping a record, our guide on how to track shared expenses shows simple ways to start.

Step 2: Use one simple formula

For each person, work out:

What you paid − your share = your balance.
Your share = total shared spending × your agreed percentage.

If your balance is positive, you paid more than your share and you're owed money. If it's negative, you owe that amount. With two people, the two balances are always the same size with opposite signs, so you only need to work out one of them.

Your “agreed percentage” is whatever split you chose, like 50/50 or an income-based 60/40. If you haven't picked one yet, the guide to splitting expenses as a couple compares the options.

Step 3: A worked example

Dora and Filip split shared costs 50/50. This month's shared spending was €1,480. Dora paid €980 of it, mostly the rent, and Filip paid €500.

  • Each person's share is €1,480 × 50% = €740.
  • Dora's balance: €980 − €740 = +€240. She's owed €240.
  • Filip's balance: €500 − €740 = −€240. He owes €240.

So Filip owes Dora €240 for the shared costs. Two more things happened this month, though.

Step 4: Add personal items paid for the other

Dora also bought Filip a €60 concert ticket that was just for him. It isn't a shared cost, so it doesn't go into the €1,480. Filip owes the full €60 on top.

Running total: €240 + €60 = €300 owed by Filip.

The same works the other way. If Filip had paid €25 for something only Dora uses, you'd subtract €25 from what he owes.

Step 5: Subtract what's already been paid back

Halfway through the month, Filip had already sent Dora €100 after a big grocery shop. That counts towards what he owes.

Final amount: €300 − €100 = €200. Filip sends Dora €200 and the month is settled.

Step 6: Choose how to settle

Once you have the number, there are three common ways to clear it:

  1. One transfer. The person who owes sends the amount. Simple and clean.
  2. Carry it forward. If the amount is small, say under €20, carry it into next month instead of sending it.
  3. Pay the next big bill. The person who owes covers the next large shared cost, like the electricity bill, and you start next month closer to even.

Whatever you choose, record it, so next month starts from a clean slate. If you carry a balance forward, write it down as an opening amount for the new month.

When the numbers don't match

Sometimes one of you gets a different answer. Before debating, check the usual suspects:

  • A payment that's missing or entered twice.
  • A personal item that slipped into the shared total, or the other way round.
  • A transfer between you that one of you forgot to count.
  • Different percentages. Make sure you both use the same split.

Nine times out of ten, it's one of these, and it takes a couple of minutes to fix when you look at the same list together.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Settling every expense separately. Lots of small transfers are easy to lose track of. Settle the month's total instead.
  • Mixing personal items into shared costs. Keep them as separate “owed in full” amounts.
  • Forgetting earlier transfers. Count every payment between you in the month.
  • Letting it build up for months. The longer you wait, the harder it is to check. Once a month works for most couples.
