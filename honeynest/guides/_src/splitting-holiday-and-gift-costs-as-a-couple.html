The festive season is one of the most expensive times of the year for many couples, and one of the easiest to argue about. There are gifts for two families, maybe a dinner to host, travel to see relatives, decorations and a few nights out. Each cost on its own feels small. Together they can add up to more than a month of groceries. This guide walks through setting one holiday budget, deciding how to split gifts for each side of the family, and handling hosting and travel, with a worked example.

Step 1: Set one holiday budget together

Start with a single number for the whole season, agreed before the shopping starts. List the categories that usually come up:

  • Gifts for your families, for each side separately.
  • Gifts for friends, colleagues and others you give to as a couple.
  • Hosting: food and drinks if you have people over.
  • Travel: fuel, train or flights to see family.
  • Decorations and the tree.
  • Going out: work parties, dinners, New Year's Eve.

Put a rough amount next to each, add them up, and check the total against what you can comfortably afford. If it's too high, trim before December, not after.

Tip: look at what you spent last year, if you can. Most couples remember the gifts and forget the food, travel and small extras.

Step 2: Decide how to handle each family's gifts

This is the part couples disagree about most, because families are different sizes and have different traditions. Three common approaches:

  • All gifts shared: every family gift comes from both of you and is split like any other shared cost. Simple, and it fits couples who see both families as “ours”.
  • Each pays for their own family: you each cover gifts for your own relatives. It feels fair when families are very different in size or traditions, but agree on it openly.
  • Shared, with an equal budget per family: you agree the same amount for each side and split it. If one of you wants to spend more on their family, they pay the extra.

Whichever you choose, agree on a rough amount per person or per family before you shop, and who buys what. It saves a lot of “I thought you were getting something for your mum”.

Step 3: Gifts for each other

Gifts for each other are usually personal: you each pay for what you give. A few couples like to agree a rough limit, so neither feels awkward about spending much more or much less. Others agree on one shared treat instead, like a weekend away. Talk about it early, since it's hard to bring up in mid-December.

Step 4: Hosting and travel

  • Hosting: food and drinks for a family dinner at your home are usually a shared cost, even if it's mostly one side of the family coming.
  • Travel: travel to see either family is often shared too, especially if you alternate where you spend the holidays each year.
  • Contributions from family: if relatives bring food or chip in, take it off the total before you split.

Step 5: A worked example

Ena and Toni plan a holiday budget of €1,200:

CostAmount
Gifts for Ena's family€250
Gifts for Toni's family (a bigger family)€350
Gifts for friends and neighbours€80
Hosting Christmas dinner€220
Travel to Toni's parents€180
Decorations and tree€70
New Year's Eve€50
Total€1,200

Gifts for each other stay personal and aren't in the table. Here's how three approaches compare:

ApproachEna paysToni pays
Everything shared 50/50€600€600
Each pays own family's gifts, the rest shared€550€650
Equal €300 per family shared, Toni pays his extra €50€575€625

In the second row, the non-family costs are €600, so €300 each. Ena adds €250 for her family and Toni €350 for his. In the third row, they share €1,150 (the total minus Toni's extra €50), so €575 each, and Toni adds his €50.

They choose the third approach. Both families get the same shared budget, and Toni can still spend a bit more on his large family without it feeling one-sided.

They also decide to plan ahead for next year: €1,200 over 12 months is €100 a month, or €50 each. Our guide to saving for a goal as a couple shows how to set that up.

Step 6: Keep track during the season

December spending happens fast, on different cards, in shops and online. A few habits help:

  1. Log each holiday cost when you pay, with who paid.
  2. Keep gifts for each other out of the shared list.
  3. Check the total against your budget once a week in December.
  4. Settle up once, in early January, not after every purchase.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • No budget until January. Agree the number before you shop.
  • Assuming you agree on family gifts. Talk about each side's traditions openly.
  • Forgetting the extras. Food, travel and wrapping paper add up.
  • Settling every purchase separately. One transfer after the holidays is much calmer.
