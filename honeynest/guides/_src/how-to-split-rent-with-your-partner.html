Rent is usually the biggest cost you share, so it's the one where an unfair split hurts most. The good news: once you've agreed on rent, most other bills follow the same rule. This guide covers the common ways to split rent, worked examples with real numbers, and the details that cause friction later, like the deposit and whose name is on the lease.

Step 1: Work out the full monthly cost

Start with the real number, not just the headline rent. Add anything that's paid with it or tied to the flat:

  • the rent itself,
  • service charges or building fees,
  • parking or storage, if you both use it,
  • any fixed amount for heating or water that the landlord adds.

Write down when it's due and who pays the landlord today. Everything else in this guide builds on that one number.

Step 2: Pick a way to split it

1. 50/50

You each pay half. It's simple and feels fair when you earn similar amounts and use the home in similar ways.

2. In proportion to income

You each pay the same percentage of what you earn. If one of you takes home much more, this keeps rent from eating a much bigger share of one person's income. The proportional bill splitting guide walks through the formula.

3. Adjusted for how you use the space

If one of you works from home in the spare room or keeps a lot of equipment there, you might agree a small adjustment, like 55/45. Keep it simple and agree it once. It's meant to feel fair, not to measure square metres.

4. One covers rent, the other covers other costs

One of you pays all the rent and the other pays bills and groceries. It's low effort, but rent is usually much bigger than everything else, so check the totals still match the split you want. Our guide to splitting bills with your partner shows how to balance it with a small monthly transfer.

Step 3: Worked examples

Nina and Leo rent a flat for €1,200 a month plus €80 in building fees, so €1,280 in total. Nina takes home €3,200 a month and Leo takes home €2,000.

MethodNina paysLeo pays
50/50€640€640
By income (about 62/38)€788€492
Adjusted for space (55/45, Nina works from home)€704€576

How the income split works: together they take home €5,200. Nina's share is €3,200 ÷ €5,200, about 61.5%, and Leo's is about 38.5%. Of €1,280, that's about €787.69 for Nina and €492.31 for Leo, rounded to €788 and €492.

At 50/50, rent takes 20% of Nina's income and 32% of Leo's. With the income split, it's about 24.6% for both. Neither answer is “right”; it depends on what feels fair to you both.

Moving into a place your partner already rents

Say Leo already rents a €900 flat and Nina moves in. Splitting it 50/50 means €450 each, and Leo's rent halves overnight. That's fine if you both agree, but talk about it openly. Some couples use the move as a moment to choose a new place together instead, so the home feels like both of yours.

Step 4: Decide how the money reaches the landlord

The landlord usually wants one payment. Three simple ways to handle it:

  1. One pays, the other transfers their share. Set up a standing order a few days before rent is due, so nobody has to remember.
  2. A joint account pays it. You both pay into a shared account and the rent comes out of it. The joint vs separate accounts guide compares the setups.
  3. Rent goes into the monthly settle-up. One pays the landlord, you record it, and it's balanced with the other shared costs at the end of the month.
Tip: record rent as a repeating monthly expense with who paid it. It's the biggest number in your shared costs, so it should never be missing from the totals.

Step 5: Sort out the deposit and the lease

These come up rarely, which is why they're easy to forget.

  • The deposit: write down who paid how much. If one of you paid it all, agree whether the other pays back their half now, over time, or not at all, and what happens when you move out.
  • The lease: it might be in one name or both. Who's on it and who pays rent are separate questions. If you want to change the names on a lease, ask your landlord what that involves where you live.
  • Moving out: agree roughly what would happen to the deposit and notice period if one of you moved out. It's an uncomfortable talk, but much easier to have early.

Step 6: When the rent goes up

Keep the same split and apply it to the new amount. If Nina and Leo's €1,280 goes up by 3%, the new total is €1,318.40. At 50/50 that's €659.20 each; with the income split it's about €811 for Nina and €507 for Leo.

A rent increase is also a good moment to check the split still feels fair, especially if either income has changed. If you're still at the planning stage, the moving in together finances checklist covers the conversations to have first.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Splitting only the headline rent. Building fees and other charges add up. Include them.
  • Forgetting about the deposit. Write down who paid it on day one.
  • Never revisiting the split. A new job, a raise or a rent increase can change what feels fair.
  • Paying rent “whenever”. Set up a standing order so the landlord is paid on time and neither of you has to chase.
