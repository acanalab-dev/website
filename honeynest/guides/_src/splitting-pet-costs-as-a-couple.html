Pets bring a lot of joy, and a steady stream of costs: food, vet visits, grooming, toys, and the occasional big bill nobody planned for. In a couple, it's often unclear who should pay, especially if the pet belonged to one of you before you moved in together. This guide walks through listing the costs, choosing a split that feels fair, planning for vet bills, and handling the tricky cases, with a worked example.

Step 1: List what your pet really costs

Pet costs come in three kinds. Writing all three down gives you a realistic picture:

  • Regular costs: food, litter, treats, toys, any monthly insurance or subscription.
  • Yearly or occasional costs: vaccinations and check-ups, grooming, flea and worm treatments, a new bed or carrier, a licence or registration where needed.
  • Unexpected costs: an illness, an injury, an emergency vet visit.

For the yearly costs, add them up and divide by 12 to get a monthly figure. That way they don't come as a surprise.

Tip: look back at the last six to twelve months of receipts or bank statements. Most people underestimate how much small pet purchases add up to.

Step 2: Decide whose pet it is, money-wise

This sounds strange, but it matters for the split. Common situations:

  • You got the pet together. Most couples treat all its costs as shared, like rent or groceries.
  • One of you had the pet before. Some couples keep it as that person's cost; others start sharing once they live together, because both now enjoy and care for the pet.
  • One of you wanted the pet much more. You might share everyday costs but agree the keener partner covers extras.

There's no right answer. Agree on one openly, and add it to your list of shared costs if it's shared. Our guide to splitting expenses as a couple covers how to decide what counts as shared.

Step 3: Choose a split

The same methods you use for other costs work here:

  • 50/50: simple, and it fits a pet you got together.
  • By income: each pays the same share of what they earn. Our proportional splitting guide shows the formula.
  • A different ratio: for example 70/30 when the pet was one partner's first, or when one of you wanted it more.
  • Split by type: share regular costs, and agree big bills separately when they happen.

Also count the time. Walks, feeding, cleaning and vet trips are work too. If one of you does almost all of it, that's worth weighing when you decide the money side. Our guide to a fair way to split household chores and costs covers how.

Step 4: A worked example

Lana and Marin share a dog, Bruno. Lana had him before they moved in together. They list his costs:

Grooming, 4 times a year€180 a year€15
Total€127.50

At 50/50, that's €63.75 each a month. Because Bruno was Lana's first, they also look at 70/30: €89.25 for Lana and €38.25 for Marin. They choose 50/50 for the regular costs, since Marin now walks Bruno most mornings.

For big unplanned bills, they agree on 70/30. When Bruno needs a €480 vet visit, Lana pays €336 and Marin €144. To avoid surprises, they also each set aside €25 a month in a small pet fund for bills like this. After a year that's €600, enough to cover a bill like Bruno's without stress.

Step 5: Plan for vet bills

The big, unexpected vet bill is where most pet arguments start. A few ways couples prepare:

  • A small pet fund that you both pay into each month, like in the example.
  • Pet insurance, if it makes sense for you. Compare what's covered carefully; policies differ a lot.
  • A limit for deciding together, for example any treatment over €300 is a joint decision.

It's also worth a calm conversation about hard decisions, like how much you'd each be willing to spend on treatment. It's much easier to talk about before you're in the vet's waiting room. For bigger emergencies beyond the pet, see our guide to a couples emergency fund.

Step 6: The tricky cases

  • Pet-sitting and kennels when you travel: treat them as part of the trip's shared costs if you're travelling together.
  • Damage at home: a chewed sofa or a scratched door. Decide whether it's a shared home cost or the pet owner's.
  • Moving out or separating: agree early who would keep the pet and who would pay for what. A short written note is enough while things are calm.
  • A second pet: revisit the whole split. What worked for one pet may not work for two.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting the yearly costs. Vaccinations and grooming add up over a year.
  • No plan for vet bills. Agree how you'll split a big bill before it arrives.
  • Ignoring the time. The partner who does the walks and vet trips is paying in effort.
  • Assuming the split never changes. Review it once a year, or when your situation changes.
