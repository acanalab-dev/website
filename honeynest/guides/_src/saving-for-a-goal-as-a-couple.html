Saving for something together, like a trip, a new sofa or a deposit on a home, is one of the nicer parts of sharing your finances. It can also cause friction if you never agreed on the amount, the date or who puts in what. This guide walks through a simple way to set a shared goal, split the contributions fairly, track progress, and adjust when life gets in the way.

Step 1: Pick one goal and make it specific

“Save more” is hard to act on. “€4,800 for a trip to Japan by next March” is easy. A good shared goal has three parts:

  • What it's for. Something you both actually want, not just one of you.
  • How much. A real number. Look up rough prices instead of guessing.
  • By when. A month and a year.

If you have several goals, rank them together and start with one or two. Spreading a little across five goals usually means none of them gets done.

Tip: give the goal a name you both like, like “Japan 2027”. It sounds small, but it makes it feel shared.

Step 2: Work out the monthly amount

Divide the goal by the number of months you have:

Goal amount ÷ months = monthly amount

For €4,800 over 16 months, that's €300 a month. If the monthly amount feels too high, you have three levers: a smaller goal, a later date, or cutting back somewhere else. Change one and run the numbers again.

Check the monthly amount fits in your budget alongside your shared costs. Our couples budget template has a “Shared goals” line for exactly this.

Step 3: Agree who puts in what

The same splits you use for everyday costs work for goals:

  • 50/50: you each put in half. Simple when incomes are similar.
  • By income: you each put in the same share of what you earn. The proportional splitting guide shows the formula.
  • Different amounts by agreement: sometimes one of you wants the goal more, or has more room this year. That's fine if you both agree openly.

For our €300 a month example, 50/50 is €150 each. With a 60/40 income split, it's €180 and €120.

It's also worth a short, calm conversation about what happens to the money if plans change, for example if you decide not to go after all. Agreeing early avoids awkward moments later.

Step 4: Keep the goal money apart

Money for a goal is easiest to protect when it isn't mixed with everyday spending. Common options:

  • a separate account or savings pot just for the goal,
  • a joint account used only for shared goals,
  • each person setting their own share aside, if you keep finances separate.

Whichever you choose, set up automatic transfers on payday so nobody has to remember. If you're still deciding how to organise your accounts, the joint vs separate accounts guide compares the options.

Step 5: A worked example, including falling behind

Ivana and Marko want €4,800 for a trip in 16 months, so they plan €300 a month, split 50/50 at €150 each.

After six months they should have €1,800, but a car repair meant they skipped two transfers, so they have €1,500. They have two simple choices:

Neither is wrong. Keeping the date costs €30 more a month; keeping the amount moves the trip back by one month. What matters is that they choose together instead of quietly falling further behind. When the trip comes, see splitting costs on a couples trip.

Step 6: Check progress once a month

Add a goal check to your monthly money talk. It takes a minute:

  1. How much is saved so far?
  2. Is that where you planned to be?
  3. If not, adjust the amount or the date now.
  4. Anything coming up that might affect next month?

Small celebrations help too. Hitting a quarter or half of the goal is worth noticing. If money talks tend to get tense, our guide on how to talk about money with your partner has a simple monthly check-in format.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Vague goals. Without an amount and a date, it's hard to know if you're on track.
  • Too many goals at once. Start with one or two.
  • Mixing goal money with spending money. It quietly disappears.
  • Not adjusting when life happens. Change the plan openly instead of giving up on it.
