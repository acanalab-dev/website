Splitting costs fairly depends on one thing: a record you both trust. Without one, each of you remembers your own payments better than your partner's, and small doubts turn into “I always pay” arguments. This guide covers what to record, three ways to keep the record, and a routine that takes a few minutes a week.

Step 1: Decide what to record

Track shared costs only. Personal spending can stay out of it, unless you both want to see it. For each shared expense, note:

  • Date, so you can see each month on its own.
  • Amount, the total you paid.
  • What it was, like groceries, rent or a dinner out.
  • Who paid. This is the part people forget, and it's the whole point.
  • A category, if you like. Home (rent, bills, groceries), Lifestyle (things you do together) and Personal is a simple set.

If you split everything the same way, you don't need to record the split on each line. Only note it for the exceptions.

Step 2: Choose where to keep it

Any tool works if you both use it. These are the three common options:

ToolGood forDownside
Shared noteGetting started today, very few expensesNo totals; you add everything up by hand
Shared spreadsheetCouples who like formulas and controlAwkward to update on a phone at the till
App built for twoLogging on the go and seeing totals instantlyBoth of you need to install it

If you go with a spreadsheet, five columns are enough: Date, What, Category, Amount and Paid by. To total what one person paid, use a formula like =SUMIF(E:E,"Sara",D:D), where column E is “Paid by” and column D is “Amount”.

Step 3: Set up repeating bills once

Rent, internet, streaming and other fixed bills are the same every month. Add them once as repeating entries instead of typing them each time. It saves effort and means the biggest costs are never forgotten. When a bill changes, update the amount once.

Not sure who should pay which bill in the first place? The guide to splitting bills with your partner covers that.

Step 4: Log as you go

The easiest record to keep is the one you update straight away. A few habits help:

  1. Log at the moment you pay. It takes ten seconds at the till and ten minutes to reconstruct from a bank statement later.
  2. Each person logs their own payments. Nobody becomes the household bookkeeper.
  3. Do a two-minute catch-up once a week, for example on Sunday evening, to add anything you missed.
  4. Split mixed receipts. If a €110 grocery shop included €20 of your own toiletries, log €90 as shared and leave the €20 out.
Tip: record shared purchases when you make them, not when the card bill arrives. It keeps each month's totals accurate.

At 50/50, each person's share is €676.50. Sara paid €357.50 more than her share, so Ivan sends Sara €357.50 and they're even. If they split 60/40 instead, Sara's share would be €811.80 and Ivan would send her €222.20.

The category totals are useful too. Seeing that Lifestyle was only €78 this month, or that groceries came to €257, makes it easier to talk about spending with real numbers instead of impressions.

Step 6: Review and settle once a month

Pick a fixed day and keep it short:

  1. Check the month is complete: rent, bills and any big purchases are all there.
  2. Look at the total and what each of you paid.
  3. Work out the difference from your agreed split and settle it with one transfer.
  4. Glance at the categories. Anything surprising is worth a quick, calm chat.

Handling the tricky cases

  • Refunds and returns: log them as a negative amount for the person who gets the money back, or edit the original entry.
  • Cash: it's the easiest to forget. Log it straight away, or round it into your weekly catch-up.
  • Big one-off purchases: agree how to split a new sofa or a holiday before you buy, and note any split that differs from your usual one.
  • Gifts for family: decide once whether gifts for each side's family are shared or personal, so you don't debate it every birthday.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Only one person tracks. It turns into a chore and the other can't check it. Both of you should be able to add and see entries.
  • Tracking every coffee. If it starts to feel like accounting, agree on a level of detail and round small amounts.
  • Settling every expense separately. One transfer a month based on the totals is far easier.
  • Letting it slide for a month. Rebuilding a month from memory is where disagreements start. The weekly catch-up prevents it.
