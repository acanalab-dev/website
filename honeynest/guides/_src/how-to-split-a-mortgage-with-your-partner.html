For most couples who own a home, the mortgage is the biggest shared cost by far. Splitting it raises questions that rent never does: who put in more for the deposit, whose name is on the loan, what happens if one of you pays more for years, and what if one of you owned the home before the other moved in. This guide walks through the common ways couples split mortgage payments, how to keep a fair record, and the conversations worth having early, with worked examples.

Step 1: Separate payments from ownership

Two different questions often get mixed up:

  • Who pays what each month: how you share the mortgage payment and other home costs.
  • Who owns what share of the home: how ownership is recorded, which may depend on the deposit, the paperwork and the rules where you live.

The two don't have to match, but you should both know how each one works for you. How ownership is recorded, and what it means if you separate, depends on your country and your paperwork, so for that part talk to a qualified professional such as a lawyer or notary. This guide focuses on the monthly money side.

Step 2: List all the home costs, not just the mortgage

The mortgage payment is only part of what a home costs. List everything:

  • the monthly mortgage payment,
  • home insurance and any building or service charges,
  • property taxes where they apply,
  • a monthly amount set aside for repairs and maintenance,
  • utilities, internet and other running costs.
Tip: setting aside a fixed amount each month for repairs makes surprise costs like a broken boiler much less stressful. Our guide to a couples emergency fund covers how to build a buffer together.

Step 3: Choose how to split the payments

50/50

You each pay half of the mortgage and home costs. Simple, and it fits couples with similar incomes who put in a similar deposit.

By income

You each pay the same share of your income. If one of you earns 60% of your combined income, they pay 60% of the home costs. Our proportional splitting guide shows the formula.

In line with the deposit

Some couples split payments in the same ratio as their deposit contributions, so payments and ownership shares stay in step. It can feel tidy, but it may be hard on the partner who earns less.

There's no right answer. What matters most is that you choose together, write it down, and keep a record of what each of you actually pays.

That way, if they ever need to work out who contributed what, the numbers are there.

When one of you owned the home first

Ana owns her flat and has a €900 monthly mortgage. Ivan moves in. Their running costs (utilities, internet, groceries) are €400 a month.

Ivan isn't on the mortgage, so they agree he pays half the running costs (€200) plus a fixed €350 a month towards housing, similar to what he'd pay in rent for his share. That's €550 a month for Ivan. Ana pays the remaining €550 of the mortgage and her €200 of running costs, so €750. Together that covers the €1,300.

They also agree what Ivan's monthly amount means: it's a contribution towards living there, like rent, not a share of ownership. If they later want Ivan to own part of the home, that's a separate step to take with professional advice. Step 5: Keep a clear record

Mortgages last years, and memories fade. Keep a simple record of:

  • what each of you put in for the deposit and buying costs,
  • how you agreed to split the monthly payments, and when it changed,
  • any big one-off payments, like an extra repayment or a renovation,
  • who paid for major repairs or improvements.

A shared note or document with dates is enough. Our guide on how to track shared expenses covers simple ways to keep it up.

Step 6: Review when things change

Revisit your split when:

  • either income changes noticeably,
  • the mortgage payment changes, for example when a fixed rate ends,
  • one of you takes parental leave or a career break,
  • you make a big extra payment or renovate.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming payments decide ownership. They're often separate. Check how it works for you.
  • No record of the deposit. It's the easiest number to forget and one of the most important.
  • Forgetting maintenance. Homes need repairs; plan for them monthly.
  • Never talking about “what if”. A calm conversation now is far easier than a hard one later.
