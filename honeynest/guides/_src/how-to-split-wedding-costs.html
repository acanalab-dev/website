Wedding costs add up fast, and they often involve more than the two of you: parents may want to help, deposits are due months ahead, and every supplier wants paying at a different time. This guide walks through how couples split wedding costs fairly, how to handle family contributions, and how to keep track of who paid what, with worked examples.

Step 1: Agree on a total budget first

Before you split anything, agree on what the whole wedding should cost. Start from what you can comfortably put in, not from the most expensive version of the day.

  • Write down roughly what each of you can contribute, and over how many months.
  • Ask family members early whether they want to contribute, and how much. Don't assume.
  • Add the two together, and that's your budget. Leave some room for surprises, as extra costs almost always appear.
Tip: decide your top two or three priorities together, like food, photos or the venue. It makes it much easier to say no to everything else.

Step 2: Handle family contributions openly

Traditions about who pays differ a lot between families and countries. Today, many couples simply ask each family what they'd like to contribute, if anything, and plan around that.

  • Ask, don't expect. Some families can't contribute, and that's fine.
  • Agree what a contribution covers. A general amount towards the day, or a specific thing like the flowers or the music.
  • Talk about expectations. Sometimes a contribution comes with opinions about the guest list or the venue. It's better to discuss that before the money arrives.
  • Keep it fair between families in a way that suits you. Equal amounts aren't required, and contributions don't need to match.

Step 3: Split what's left between the two of you

Once you know what families are contributing, the rest is yours to split. The same methods that work for everyday costs work here.

50/50

You each pay half. Simple, and it works well when your incomes and savings are similar.

By income

You each pay the same share of what you earn. If one of you earns more, this can feel fairer. The proportional splitting guide shows the formula.

By category

Each of you takes on certain parts, like the venue and food for one, and the photographer, music and rings for the other. Check the totals still match the split you want, because some categories are much bigger than others.

Step 4: A worked example

Mia and Noah plan a wedding with a total budget of €18,000. Mia's parents offer €4,000 and Noah's parents offer €3,000, so families cover €7,000 and the couple covers €11,000.

Spreading it over the months before the wedding turns a big number into a regular amount you can plan around, like any other shared cost.

When guest lists are uneven

If one side of the family is much bigger, some couples split the per-guest costs by guest count. Say catering is €90 per guest for 100 guests, so €9,000. With 60 guests from Mia's side and 40 from Noah's, catering would split €5,400 and €3,600. It's optional, but it can help when families are contributing and the numbers are very uneven.

Step 5: Track every deposit and payment

Wedding payments are spread over many months and often paid from different cards. Without a record, it's hard to know where you stand. For each payment, note:

  • the supplier and what it's for,
  • the amount and date,
  • who paid it, including family members,
  • what's still left to pay and when it's due.

Then settle up between the two of you regularly, for example once a month. If Mia paid a €2,000 venue deposit and Noah paid an €800 photographer deposit, together that's €2,800. At 50/50 each share is €1,400, so Noah sends Mia €600 and you're even.

Step 6: Plan for after the wedding

A few costs often show up after the day itself: final supplier balances, thank-you cards, and anything that went over budget. Keep them on the same list until everything is paid.

Once the wedding is done, it's a good moment to set up your shared finances for married life. The budgeting for newlyweds guide covers the first budget, shared goals and tidying up wedding costs.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Planning before agreeing on a total. It's much harder to cut back than to add later.
  • Assuming family will contribute. Ask early, and plan only with amounts that have been offered.
  • Forgetting small costs. Alterations, transport, tips and stationery add up.
  • Not tracking who paid what. Months of deposits from different cards are easy to lose track of.
