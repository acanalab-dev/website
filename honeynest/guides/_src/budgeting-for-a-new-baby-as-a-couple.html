A baby changes almost every line of a couple's budget. There are things to buy before the birth, new monthly costs afterwards, often a drop in income during leave, and later childcare. It's a lot to plan while also getting ready for a big life change. This guide walks through listing the one-off and monthly costs, building a baby budget together, deciding how to split it, and planning for the months ahead, with a worked example.

Step 1: List the one-off costs

Many of the bigger purchases happen before the baby arrives. Write them down with a rough price for each:

  • Getting around: pram or buggy, car seat, baby carrier.
  • Sleeping: cot or crib, mattress, bedding.
  • Everyday care: changing mat, bath, towels, a first set of clothes.
  • Feeding: whatever your plan needs, such as bottles or a feeding chair.
  • Home changes: a room or corner to set up, storage, a bigger washing load.

Prices vary a lot. Second-hand items, hand-me-downs and gifts from family can make a big difference, so decide which items you'd happily get used and which you'd rather buy new.

Tip: share a simple wish list with family who want to help. It avoids three of the same thing and makes gifts more useful.

Step 2: Estimate the new monthly costs

After the birth, some costs come back every month:

  • nappies and wipes,
  • formula or food, depending on your plans,
  • clothes as the baby grows,
  • extra laundry, heating and electricity,
  • later, childcare, which is often the biggest new cost.

Use rough numbers to start and adjust after the first month or two, when you know your real spending.

Step 3: Plan for income changes

Income often drops while one or both of you are on leave. Leave pay and how long it lasts depend on your country and employer, so check with your employer and the official sources where you live. Once you know the numbers, our guide on splitting costs during parental leave shows how to adjust your split during that time.

Step 4: A worked example

Klara and Filip are five months from their due date. Klara earns 55% of their combined income, and they already split shared costs 55/45.

Total€1,150

Saved over five months, that's €1,150 ÷ 5 = €230 a month. At 55/45, Klara puts in €126.50 and Filip €103.50 each month.

New monthly costs after the birth: they estimate €200 a month: €60 for nappies and wipes, €70 for feeding, €30 for clothes, €20 for extra laundry and energy, and €20 for everything else. Their existing shared costs are €2,300, so the new total is €2,500. At 55/45, that's €1,375 for Klara and €1,125 for Filip, before any change for parental leave.

They also note that childcare, which they estimate at €400 a month, will start when Klara goes back to work. They add it to their plan now so it isn't a surprise.

Step 5: Decide how to split baby costs

Most couples treat baby costs as fully shared, like rent or groceries. The question is which split to use:

  • Your usual split: 50/50 or by income, whatever you use for other shared costs.
  • Adjusted for leave: during parental leave, many couples change the split so the parent on leave still has money of their own.
  • Equal money left over: some couples aim for both partners to have the same personal amount after all shared costs, which can feel fairest once a child arrives.

Our guide on splitting costs when one partner earns much more compares these options with numbers.

Step 6: Talk about the bigger decisions

A baby often brings bigger questions that affect your budget for years, not months. You don't need to answer them all at once, but it helps to name them early:

  • Space: is your home big enough for now, and for the next few years? A move changes rent or mortgage, deposits and moving costs.
  • Transport: does your car fit a car seat and a pram, or will you need to change it?
  • Leave and work: who takes how much leave, and will either of you change hours afterwards?
  • Personal money: how much will each of you keep for yourselves once costs go up?

Write down what you agree and the date, and come back to it when plans change.

Step 7: Build a buffer

Babies come with surprises: a sudden growth spurt in clothes, a broken pram wheel, extra costs if plans change. A buffer helps you handle them calmly. Our guide to a couples emergency fund shows how to set one up together. If the budget is tight, it's fine to pause other savings goals for a while and restart them later.

Step 8: Review every month for the first year

Your first guesses will be off, and that's normal. Check in monthly:

  1. What did baby costs really come to this month?
  2. Does your split still work, especially during leave?
  3. What's coming up: a bigger car seat, childcare, a return to work?

Our monthly money check-in template keeps it to about 20 minutes.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Only planning the big purchases. The monthly costs add up faster.
  • Forgetting childcare. Plan for it before the return to work.
  • Buying everything new by default. Decide item by item.
  • Not adjusting the split during leave. The parent on leave still needs money of their own.

This guide shares general tips for organising shared costs. It isn't financial, tax, legal or medical advice, and it doesn't cover leave pay or benefits, which differ by country and employer. For advice about your situation, talk to your employer, the official sources where you live, or a qualified professional.
