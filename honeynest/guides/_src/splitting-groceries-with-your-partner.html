Groceries look like a small cost, but they're the one you pay most often, and they're full of grey areas. Is the oat milk only one of you drinks shared? What about the snacks for work, or the wine? This guide covers how to decide what's shared, five simple ways to split it, how to handle mixed receipts, and a worked month with real numbers.

Step 1: Agree what counts as shared

Most grocery friction comes from this, not from the math. Sort items into two groups once, and you won't need to discuss them every week.

  • Usually shared: food you cook and eat together, staples like bread, milk, oil, coffee and spices, and household supplies like cleaning products, toilet paper and bin bags.
  • Often personal: things only one of you uses, like lunches and snacks for work, supplements, special diet products only one of you eats, alcohol if only one of you drinks, and toiletries.

Write down the grey areas and decide them once. For example: “coffee is shared, energy drinks are personal”.

Tip: agree a small amount below which you don't bother separating personal items, like €3. It saves a lot of receipt-picking.

Step 2: Pick a way to split

1. Split everything 50/50

All grocery shops are shared, no matter who eats what. It's the easiest option and works well if you eat mostly the same things.

2. Shared basics, personal extras

Shared items are split; personal items are paid by whoever uses them. It's a bit more effort but feels fair when your tastes differ.

3. Take turns

You alternate who pays for the weekly shop. It's simple, but shops vary in size, so check once a month that it roughly evens out.

4. A grocery pot

You both put a set amount each month into a joint account or a shared card used only for groceries. The joint vs separate accounts guide explains how a shared account works.

5. Adjusted split

If one of you eats noticeably more, or incomes differ a lot, you might agree on something like 55/45 instead of 50/50. Keep it simple and agree it once. The proportional splitting guide shows how to work out an income-based split.

Step 3: Handle mixed receipts

Most shops will include a few personal items. Two simple ways to deal with them:

  1. Take the personal items off the total. If a €84 shop included €12 of your own things, log €72 as shared.
  2. Pay for personal items separately at the till. Some couples split the basket into two payments. It takes a minute but keeps the record clean.

Whichever you choose, log each shop with who paid. Our guide on how to track shared expenses covers simple ways to keep that record.

Eva paid €171 of shared groceries (€72 + €47 + €52) and Jan paid €181 (€65 + €78 + €38).

At 50/50, each person's share is €176. Eva paid €5 less than her share, so she sends Jan €5. Taking turns evened out almost perfectly this month.

At 55/45, because Jan eats more, Jan's share is €193.60 and Eva's is €158.40. Eva paid €12.60 more than her share, so Jan sends Eva €12.60.

Small differences like these are a good reason to settle groceries together with your other shared costs once a month, instead of after every shop.

Step 5: Set a rough grocery budget

Once you've tracked a couple of months, you'll know your typical spend. Eva and Jan's €352 month suggests a budget of around €360, which is about €83 a week (€360 × 12 ÷ 52).

A budget isn't about spending less at all costs. It gives you a shared number to plan around, and it makes a surprising month easy to spot. If you're putting groceries in a shared pot, it's also the amount to pay in.

Step 6: Keep it easy week to week

  • Keep one shared shopping list, so you don't both buy the same things.
  • Log each shop when you pay, with the amount and who paid. It takes seconds.
  • Round small amounts. Nobody needs to split a €1.20 pack of gum.
  • Settle once a month, together with your other shared costs.
  • Revisit the method if your habits change, for example a new diet, working from home or a new job with a canteen.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Never agreeing what's shared. Every unclear item becomes a small negotiation. Decide the grey areas once.
  • Splitting every receipt to the cent. If it starts to feel like accounting, use a threshold and round.
  • Forgetting household supplies. Cleaning products and toilet paper are often bought with groceries and are usually shared.
  • Only remembering your own shops. Without a shared record, each of you feels like you pay more. Write it down.
