A couples emergency fund: how much and who pays

A shared emergency fund turns a broken boiler or a lost job from a crisis into an inconvenience. Here's how to agree what it's for, set a target, split the contributions and refill it after you use it, with a worked example.

A boiler breaks, a car fails its inspection, or one of you loses a job. A shared emergency fund turns moments like these from a crisis into an inconvenience. For couples it also raises questions a single person doesn't face: how big should it be, who pays in, and what counts as an emergency for both of you? This guide walks through setting a target, splitting the contributions, agreeing the rules, and refilling it after you use it, with a worked example.

Step 1: Agree what the fund is for

An emergency fund is money set aside for costs that are unexpected and necessary. Before you decide on an amount, agree on what it covers. A simple test: is it urgent, necessary and unplanned?

  • Usually yes: an urgent home repair, a broken shared appliance, a car repair you need to get to work, covering shared costs if one of you loses income for a while.
  • Usually no: a holiday, a sale, a new phone because the old one is slow, a planned cost you knew was coming.
  • Agree case by case: a vet bill, a family emergency, a medical cost. Talk about these before they happen.

Known costs that come once a year, like insurance or a car service, belong in your budget rather than your emergency fund. Our couples budget template has a line for them.

Step 2: Work out a target

A common starting point is to cover a few months of your essential shared costs: rent or mortgage, bills, groceries and transport. Many people aim for somewhere between three and six months, but the right number depends on your situation. You might want more if your income is irregular, if one of you is self-employed, or if you rely on one income. You might be comfortable with less if you both have very stable jobs and your own personal savings.

To work out your number:

  1. Add up your essential shared costs for one month.
  2. Multiply by the number of months you both feel comfortable with.
  3. Subtract anything you already have set aside for emergencies.
Tip: if the full target feels out of reach, start with a first milestone, like one month of essential costs. Reaching it quickly is motivating, and you can raise the target later.

Step 3: Decide who pays in

Use the same rule you use for other shared costs, or agree a different one for this:

  • 50/50: you each put in the same amount. Simple when incomes are similar.
  • By income: you each put in the same share of what you earn. Our proportional splitting guide shows the formula.
  • One person tops up: if one of you already has personal savings, you can agree to count part of them, as long as you both say so openly.

Also agree what happens to the money if you separate. A sentence like “we each get back what we put in” or “we split it equally” is enough, written down while things are calm.

Step 4: A worked example

Mara and Ivo's essential shared costs are €2,400 a month: €1,300 rent, €300 bills, €600 groceries and €200 transport. They want three months covered, so their target is €2,400 × 3 = €7,200.

They already have €1,200 in a shared savings account, so €6,000 is left to save. They'd like to get there in 20 months, so €6,000 ÷ 20 = €300 a month.

Mara earns 60% of their combined income, so they choose by income: €180 and €120. Their first milestone, one month of costs (€2,400), takes four months: €1,200 already saved plus 4 × €300.

Eight months in, their car needs a €900 repair. They have €1,200 + 8 × €300 = €3,600 saved, so they pay the repair from the fund and have €2,700 left. To get back on track they can save €300 more a month for three months, or keep the same amount and reach the target three months later. Either is fine, as long as they choose together.

Step 5: Keep it separate and easy to reach

Keep the fund apart from your everyday spending, so it doesn't slowly disappear into groceries and dinners. Many couples use a separate shared savings account or pot that they can reach quickly when they need it. Set up automatic transfers on payday so nobody has to remember.

Which account or product to use depends on your situation and country. If you want advice on that, talk to your bank or a qualified professional. Our joint vs separate accounts guide covers how couples organise shared money in general.

Step 6: Agree the rules for using it

  1. Tell each other before you use it, or within a day in a real emergency.
  2. Agree a limit above which you decide together, for example anything over €200.
  3. Write down each withdrawal: date, amount and what it was for.
  4. Agree how you'll refill it, for example by raising the monthly amount for a few months.

If one of you loses income, agree in advance how long the fund covers their share of shared costs. That one conversation removes a lot of stress if it ever happens.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using it for non-emergencies. A planned holiday isn't an emergency, however tempting.
  • No agreement on what counts. Talk through the grey areas before you need to.
  • Never refilling it. After a withdrawal, make a plan to get back to the target.
  • Setting a target so big you give up. Start with a first milestone and build from there.

Frequently asked questions

How big should a couples emergency fund be?

Many people aim for a few months of essential shared costs, often somewhere between three and six, but the right amount depends on your situation, such as how stable your incomes are.

Who should pay into a shared emergency fund?

Use the same rule as your other shared costs: 50/50 if your incomes are similar, or by income so you each put in the same share of what you earn.

What counts as an emergency?

Costs that are urgent, necessary and unplanned, like an urgent home or car repair, or covering shared costs if one of you loses income. Agree on the grey areas in advance.

What happens to the emergency fund if we break up?

Agree that while things are calm, for example that you each get back what you put in or split it equally, and write it down.
