Couples budget template: free download and a filled-in example

A couples budget doesn't need to be complicated. One page with your incomes, your shared costs, how you split them and what's left for each of you covers most of what you need. This guide gives you a free template you can download, shows it filled in with real numbers, and explains how to keep it up to date in a few minutes a month.

The template

Download the couples budget template (CSV). It opens in Excel, Google Sheets and Numbers, and the totals and split are already worked out with formulas. Just replace the zeros with your own numbers.

It has four parts:

  1. Income: what each of you takes home each month, and each person's share of the total.
  2. Shared costs: a planned and an actual amount for each category you share.
  3. Split: the percentage each of you pays, and what that comes to.
  4. What's left: how much each of you keeps for personal spending.
Tip: enter the split as a percentage, like 50% or 58%, in the “Split %” row for Partner 1. Partner 2's percentage fills in automatically.

Step 1: Fill in your incomes

Use take-home pay, the amount that actually arrives in your account each month. If one of you has an income that varies, use a typical or slightly cautious month. The template shows each person's share of your combined income, which you'll need if you split costs by income.

Step 2: List your shared costs

Go through the categories and put in a planned monthly amount for each. The template includes:

  • Rent or mortgage, utilities and internet: your fixed home costs.
  • Groceries and household: food you share, cleaning products and other basics.
  • Eating out and date nights: what you do together.
  • Subscriptions: streaming and other services you both use.
  • Yearly costs: things like insurance or gifts that come once or twice a year. Divide the yearly total by 12 and plan for it monthly.
  • Buffer: a small amount for the unexpected, so one surprise bill doesn't throw off the month.
  • Shared goals: money you put aside together, for example for a holiday.

Rename or add rows to match your life. Not sure what counts as shared? The guide to splitting bills with your partner walks through it bill by bill.

Step 3: A filled-in example

Sofia takes home €2,900 a month and Daniel €2,100, so €5,000 together. Sofia earns 58% of their combined income. Here's their planned month:

At 50/50, each pays €1,047.50. Sofia has €1,852.50 left for personal spending and Daniel has €1,052.50.

Split by income (58/42), Sofia pays €1,215.10 and Daniel pays €879.90. Sofia has €1,684.90 left and Daniel has €1,220.10.

Seeing both versions side by side makes the choice much easier to talk about. The proportional splitting guide explains the income-based method in more detail.

Step 4: Compare plan and actual each month

A budget is most useful when you check it against what really happened. At the end of the month, fill in the “Actual” column. For Sofia and Daniel:

CategoryPlannedActualDifference
Groceries and household€480€530+€50
Eating out and date nights€120€95−€25
Utilities€140€165+€25

Everything else matched the plan, so the month came in €50 over, and the €50 buffer covered it exactly. Nothing to worry about, but if groceries keep landing around €530, it's worth raising the plan instead of feeling “over budget” every month.

To fill in the “Actual” column, you need a record of what you spent. Our guide on how to track shared expenses covers simple ways to keep one.

Step 5: Keep it going

  • Check it once a month, together, in about fifteen minutes. Settle up at the same time.
  • Adjust the plan, not just the spending. A plan that never matches reality stops being useful.
  • Update incomes when they change, especially if you split costs by income.
  • Revisit categories every few months. New subscriptions and new habits creep in.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Planning too tightly. A budget with no buffer breaks at the first surprise.
  • Forgetting yearly costs. They're the bills that make a normal month feel expensive.
  • Only one person looks at it. A shared budget works when you both understand it.
  • Using the budget to judge each other. It's a tool for planning together, not for keeping score.

Frequently asked questions

What should a couples budget include?

Each partner's take-home income, your shared costs by category, how you split them, what's left for each of you, and room for yearly costs and a small buffer.

Does the template work in Google Sheets?

Yes. The template is a CSV file that opens in Google Sheets, Excel and Numbers, with the totals and split worked out by formulas.

Should we budget personal spending too?

That's up to you. Many couples budget shared costs together and leave each person's remaining money to them.

How often should we update our budget?

Check it once a month against what you actually spent, and update incomes and categories whenever something changes.
