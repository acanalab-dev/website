Splitting costs during parental leave

Parental leave usually means one income drops just as new costs arrive. Here's how to plan the numbers early, adjust your split fairly, keep personal money for both of you, and plan the return to work, with a worked example.

Parental leave changes a couple's money in two ways at once: one income usually drops, and new costs arrive with the baby. A split that felt fair before can suddenly leave the parent on leave with very little of their own, right when they're doing the most unpaid work. This guide walks through planning the numbers before leave starts, three ways to adjust your split, keeping personal money for both of you, and what to change when you go back to work, with a worked example.

Step 1: Run the numbers before leave starts

The best time to talk about money during parental leave is a few months before it begins. Write down:

  • What each of you will receive during leave. Leave pay and how long it lasts depend on your country and employer. Check with your employer and the official sources where you live rather than guessing.
  • Any change for the other partner, such as shorter hours or their own leave.
  • New regular costs: nappies, formula, clothes, extra laundry and heating, and later childcare.
  • One-off costs: a pram, a cot, a car seat. Plan these before the leave if you can. Our guide to budgeting for a new baby has a full list.

Then compare your combined income during leave with your shared costs. If they don't fit, you'll know early enough to adjust.

Tip: try living on your “leave budget” for a month or two before the baby arrives, and put the difference aside. It tests the plan and builds a small cushion at the same time.

Step 2: Choose how to adjust your split

Most couples change their split during leave. Three common options:

Keep 50/50

Simple, but if one income drops a lot, the partner on leave can end up paying most of their reduced income towards shared costs. It only works if the drop is small.

Split by income

Each of you pays the same share of what you receive. Because the shares are based on income, they adjust automatically when one income falls. Our proportional splitting guide shows the formula.

Equal money left over

You aim for both of you to have the same amount left after shared costs. The partner still working pays more, and the partner on leave keeps the same personal money. Many couples feel this fits best, since raising a child is a shared job, whoever is on leave.

Step 3: A worked example

Before leave, Sara takes home €2,600 a month and Tin €2,400. Their shared costs are €2,500 a month, split by income: Sara pays €1,300 and Tin €1,200, leaving them €1,300 and €1,200.

During Sara's leave, she receives €1,500 a month in this example. Baby costs add about €300 a month, so shared costs rise to €2,800. Their combined income is now €1,500 + €2,400 = €3,900.

By income, Sara receives €1,500 ÷ €3,900, about 38% of their income, so she pays about 38% of €2,800. For “equal left over”, they have €3,900 − €2,800 = €1,100 left together, so €550 each, and each pays the rest of their income towards shared costs.

At 50/50, Sara would have just €100 a month of her own. They choose equal money left over for the leave period, and agree to look at it again when Sara goes back to work.

Step 4: Keep personal money for both of you

Whatever split you choose, the parent on leave should still have money that's theirs to spend without asking. Depending on someone else for every coffee or haircut can feel uncomfortable, even in a happy relationship. Agree a personal amount for each of you that needs no explaining, and keep it during leave.

If you have a joint account, you might each get the same monthly “own money” transfer. Our joint vs separate accounts guide compares the setups.

Step 5: Adjust savings goals and the buffer

  • Slow down or pause savings goals during leave if the budget is tight. Moving a target date is fine.
  • Keep a buffer for surprises. Our guide to a couples emergency fund shows how to build one before the leave.
  • Agree what the buffer is for, so neither of you feels guilty using it for a real need.

Step 6: Plan for going back to work

When leave ends, the picture changes again. Income usually rises, but childcare can be one of the biggest new costs. Before the return:

  1. Add childcare to your shared costs, and decide how to split it like any other shared cost.
  2. Check whether either of you changes hours, and update incomes.
  3. Choose your split again, using your new numbers.
  4. Restart any goals you paused.

A short monthly check-in helps through all of this. Our monthly money check-in template has a simple agenda.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting until leave starts. Run the numbers a few months ahead.
  • Keeping 50/50 by default. It can leave the parent on leave with almost nothing.
  • No personal money for the parent on leave. Agree an amount for both of you.
  • Forgetting childcare. Plan for it before the return to work.

This guide shares general tips for organising shared costs. It isn't financial, tax or legal advice, and it doesn't cover leave pay or entitlements, which differ by country and employer. For advice about your situation, talk to your employer, the official sources where you live, or a qualified professional.

Frequently asked questions

How should couples split costs during parental leave?

Many couples move away from 50/50: they split by income, so shares adjust when one income drops, or aim for both to have the same money left over after shared costs.

Should the parent on leave still have personal money?

Most couples find it helps to agree a personal amount for each of you that needs no explaining, and to keep it during leave.

When should we plan the money for parental leave?

A few months before it starts, so you can compare your leave income with your shared costs and adjust early.

What changes when we go back to work?

Income usually rises but childcare often becomes a big new cost, so add it to shared costs and choose your split again.
