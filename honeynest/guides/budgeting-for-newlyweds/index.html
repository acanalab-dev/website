Budgeting for newlyweds: a simple first budget

After the wedding, money often changes more than couples expect. Here's a simple first budget for newlyweds: the full picture, leftover wedding costs, how much to combine, a couple of shared goals, and a worked example with real numbers.

After the wedding, money often changes more than couples expect. There may be leftover wedding costs, new shared goals, and the question of whether to combine your finances more than before. This guide walks through a simple first budget for newlyweds: getting the full picture, tidying up after the wedding, choosing how to combine, and setting a few shared goals, with a worked example.

Step 1: Put the full picture on the table

Even if you've lived together for years, marriage is a good moment to look at everything together once. Sit down with a coffee and list:

  • Income: what each of you takes home each month.
  • Regular costs: rent or mortgage, bills, groceries, subscriptions, transport.
  • Debts: any loans or card balances, how much is left and the monthly payment.
  • Accounts: where your money sits today, joint and personal.
  • Commitments: anything either of you pays for family, or plans to.

This isn't about judging each other's past choices. It's about both of you seeing the same numbers. If money talks feel awkward, our guide on how to talk about money with your partner has tips for keeping it relaxed.

Step 2: Tidy up the wedding costs

Weddings often leave a few loose ends. Before you plan the future, close these out. (Still planning the day? See how to split wedding costs.)

  • Final payments still due to venues, photographers or other suppliers.
  • Any wedding spending on a credit card, and a plan for clearing it.
  • Money you received as gifts, and what you want to use it for.
  • Small costs like thank-you cards and returning hired items.
Tip: write the leftover wedding costs down as one list with a total. A single number is much easier to plan around than a feeling that “the wedding was expensive”.

Step 3: Decide how much to combine

There's no rule that married couples must merge everything. Common setups are fully separate accounts, a joint account for shared costs plus a personal account each, or fully joint finances. Our joint vs separate accounts guide compares them with examples.

Whatever you choose, agree on two things now: which costs are shared, and how you split them. If your incomes differ, the proportional splitting guide shows how to split by income.

Step 4: Build your first budget together

Keep it to one page: incomes, shared costs, any shared goals, how you split them, and what's left for each of you. Our couples budget template has a free download with the formulas already set up.

A worked example

Lea takes home €2,600 a month and Tomas takes home €2,400, so €5,000 together. Lea earns 52% of the total. After the wedding they have:

  • €2,200 a month of shared costs: rent, bills, groceries, transport and some date nights.
  • €1,800 left on a credit card from wedding costs, which they want to clear in six months: €300 a month.
  • A goal of a €3,000 buffer for unexpected costs within a year: €250 a month.

That's €2,200 + €300 + €250 = €2,750 a month to cover together.

Step 5: Pick one or two shared goals

Newlyweds often have a long wish list: a holiday, a bigger home, a car, a buffer for surprises. Trying to do everything at once usually means nothing moves. Pick one or two goals and give each a number and a date.

The math is simple: divide the amount by the number of months. A €2,400 holiday in 12 months is €200 a month, or €100 each at 50/50. Then add it to your budget as a line like any other shared cost.

Review the goals every few months. Plans change, and that's fine. Our guide to saving for a goal as a couple goes into more detail.

Step 6: Check the practical admin

Marriage can come with a bit of paperwork. Depending on your situation:

  • If either of you changed your name or address, update it with your bank, employer and providers.
  • Check whose name is on shared bills and contracts, and whether you want that to change.
  • Ask your bank, insurer or employer whether anything changes for you after marriage. Rules differ between countries, so a qualified professional is the right person for specific questions.

Step 7: Make a monthly money date

A short monthly check-in keeps your first year on track. Look at what you spent, settle up if you split costs, check progress on your goals, and talk about anything coming up next month. Fifteen minutes is usually enough.

To see what you actually spent, you need a shared record. Our guide on how to track shared expenses covers simple ways to keep one.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming you agree. Being married doesn't mean you have the same money habits. Talk it through.
  • Letting wedding costs linger. Leftover balances are easier to clear with a plan and a date.
  • Too many goals at once. One or two clear goals move faster than ten vague ones.
  • No personal money. Even couples who combine everything are usually happier with an amount each that needs no explaining.

This guide shares general tips for organising shared costs. It isn't financial, tax or legal advice. For advice about your situation, talk to a qualified professional.

Frequently asked questions

How should newlyweds start budgeting?

Start by listing both incomes, regular costs, debts and accounts together. Then tidy up any leftover wedding costs, decide which costs are shared and how to split them, and put it all on one page.

Do married couples have to combine all their money?

No. Many married couples keep a joint account for shared costs and a personal account each. Fully separate and fully joint setups also work if you both agree.

How do we plan for a shared goal?

Give it an amount and a date, divide the amount by the number of months, and add that as a line in your budget. For example, €2,400 in 12 months is €200 a month.

How often should newlyweds review their budget?

A short monthly check-in works well, with a bigger review when something changes, like a new job or a move.
