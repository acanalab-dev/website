How to split expenses as a couple: 4 fair methods

Splitting money as a couple gets easier once you agree on what's shared, pick a method that feels fair to both of you, and settle up on a schedule. Here are the four common methods, a worked example with real numbers, and a routine that takes ten minutes a month.

There's no single right way to split money in a relationship. What works is a method you both understand, that feels fair to both of you, and that's easy enough to keep up every month. This guide walks through the four common methods, how to choose, and a simple routine to keep track without arguments.

Step 1: Decide what counts as shared

Before you pick a split, agree on which costs are “ours” and which are “mine”. Most disagreements come from this, not from the math. A useful way to sort them is into three groups:

  • Home: the costs of running your life together, like rent or mortgage, utilities, internet, groceries, household supplies and repairs.
  • Lifestyle: things you do together, like dinners out, date nights, trips, gifts for family and shared subscriptions.
  • Personal: things only one of you uses, like your own clothes, hobbies, gym, phone plan or a coffee on the way to work.

Home and Lifestyle are usually split. Personal usually isn't. Write down the grey areas now (a car only one of you drives, a pet, gifts for one person's family) and decide them once, so you don't renegotiate every time.

Tip: if one of you earns much more and you want to keep things simple, you can agree that Lifestyle costs are split differently from Home costs. For example, Home 50/50 and the higher earner covers more of the trips.

Step 2: Pick a method

These are the four methods couples use most. None is “correct”; each trades simplicity against how closely it reflects your situation.

1. 50/50

Every shared cost is split equally. It's the easiest to understand and track, and it works well when you earn similar amounts and like similar things.

It can feel unfair when incomes differ a lot: €900 of shared costs is a much bigger share of a €2,000 income than of a €4,500 one.

2. Proportional to income

Each of you pays the same percentage of your income. If you earn €3,000 and your partner earns €2,000, you'd pay 60% of shared costs and they'd pay 40%. We walk through the math with examples in the proportional bill splitting guide.

Many couples find it fairer when incomes differ. The trade-off is that you need to share income numbers and update them when they change.

3. Split by category

Instead of splitting each bill, each of you owns some bills. One pays rent, the other pays groceries and utilities. It's low effort because there's nothing to calculate per purchase.

The catch is that bills grow at different speeds. Check every few months that the totals still feel balanced.

4. A shared pot

You both put a fixed amount into a joint account or a shared card each month, and shared costs come out of it. What you each put in can be equal or proportional. Anything not shared stays in your own account.

It's clean once it's set up, but you still need to agree on how much goes in and what to do when the pot runs short or has money left.

Step 3: A worked example

Ana and Luka live together. In one month, their shared costs were:

  • Rent: €1,000, paid by Ana
  • Groceries: €420, of which Ana paid €160 and Luka paid €260
  • Utilities and internet: €130, paid by Luka
  • Two dinners out: €90, paid by Luka

Total shared spending is €1,640. Ana paid €1,160 and Luka paid €480.

With a 50/50 split, each person's share is €820. Ana paid €340 more than her share, so Luka would pay Ana €340 to even it out.

With a 60/40 split (Ana earns more), Ana's share is €984 and Luka's is €656. Ana paid €176 more than her share, so Luka would pay Ana €176.

The trick is to settle the difference, not every purchase. One transfer at the end of the month replaces dozens of small ones.

Step 4: Track it without nagging

Whatever method you pick, you need a record of who paid what. Without one, people remember their own payments better than their partner's, and that's where “I always pay” arguments come from.

  1. Log as you go. Add each shared cost when you pay it. It takes seconds then and minutes to reconstruct later.
  2. Note who paid. The amount alone isn't enough. The whole point is to compare.
  3. Set up repeating bills once. Rent, internet and subscriptions are the same each month, so record them as recurring instead of typing them every time.
  4. Keep personal spending separate. Mixing it in makes the shared totals meaningless.

A shared note or spreadsheet can work if you're both disciplined about it. An app built for two people saves you the formulas.

Step 5: Settle up on a schedule

Pick a regular moment to look at the numbers together, for example the first weekend of the month. Keep it short:

  1. Look at the total shared spending and what each of you paid.
  2. Compare that with your agreed split and work out the difference.
  3. Settle with one transfer, or let the person who paid less cover the next big shared cost.
  4. Ask one question: does this split still feel fair? If not, adjust it now, not in the middle of an argument.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Splitting everything to the cent. If it starts to feel like accounting, round small amounts and settle the big picture.
  • Never revisiting the method. A new job, a child or a move changes what's fair. Review it once or twice a year.
  • Hidden personal spending on the shared card. Agree on what goes on it, and keep personal costs off it.
  • Keeping score emotionally. The numbers are there to stop the score-keeping, not to win it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fairest way to split expenses as a couple?

There's no single fairest way. 50/50 works well when incomes are similar, and splitting in proportion to income is often seen as fairer when incomes differ a lot. The best method is one you both agree on and review from time to time.

Should couples split everything 50/50?

Not necessarily. Many couples split shared costs like rent and groceries but keep personal spending separate, and some use a proportional split when one earns noticeably more.

How often should couples settle up?

Once a month is common and easy to remember. Settle the difference between what each of you paid and your agreed split with one transfer, instead of paying each other back for every purchase.

Do we need a joint bank account to split expenses?

No. You can each pay bills from your own accounts and track who paid what, then settle the difference. A joint account for shared costs is one option, not a requirement.
