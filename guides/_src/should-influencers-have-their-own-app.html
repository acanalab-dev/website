The short answer

Some influencers should have their own app, and many shouldn't. An app makes sense when your audience comes to you for something they want every day, like motivation, calm, workouts or recipes, and when you're ready to mention it regularly in your content. It makes less sense if your followers mainly watch you for entertainment, or if you'd rather sell something once and move on.

The cost and effort have changed a lot, too. A few years ago, your own app meant hiring developers and paying a large bill upfront. Today there are lighter options, so the real question is less "can I afford an app?" and more "would my followers actually open it?"

This guide walks through the pros, the cons, a quick self-check and the main ways to get an app, so you can decide honestly.

The case for your own app

  • You own the relationship. On social platforms, an algorithm decides who sees your posts. An app on someone's Home Screen is a direct line to them that no feed change can take away.
  • It's there every day. A morning notification or a widget puts your voice in front of followers daily, without you making a new post.
  • It can earn monthly. Most apps earn through subscriptions. Unlike a one-off sale, every new subscriber adds to a base that carries over into the next month.
  • It's a premium brand signal. "Download my app" lands differently from "click the link in my bio". It can strengthen your position with followers and with brands.
  • It fits naturally in some niches. For mindset, wellness, fitness and self-care creators, an app is often just a daily version of what you already share, not a hard sell. See how to monetize a wellness audience for the other options.

The case against

  • Most apps get deleted. If an app isn't useful every day, people try it once and forget it. A daily habit is what keeps an app on a phone.
  • It needs ongoing promotion. An app won't grow just because it exists. You'll need to mention it regularly, in a way that fits your content.
  • Custom apps are expensive. Building from scratch can mean a large upfront cost, months of work and paying again for updates when phones and operating systems change.
  • Fees take a share. App stores take a commission on subscriptions, and depending on how you get the app, a provider or platform may too. Compare what you actually keep.
  • It may not fit your niche. A comedy, gaming or car-review channel rarely has a daily-use app that followers would pay for. Forcing it wastes effort.

A quick self-check: is an app right for you?

Answer these honestly. The more "yes" answers, the stronger the case.

  1. Do followers come to you for something they want daily? Encouragement, a workout, a recipe idea, a moment of calm.
  2. Do people already ask for "more" of it? Comments like "I screenshot these every morning" or "I wish I had this on my phone" are strong signals.
  3. Is your audience mostly on iPhone or Android? Many app options start with one platform. Check your audience insights if your platform shows device data.
  4. Will you mention it regularly? A few natural mentions a month, in stories or posts, is usually enough. None at all usually isn't.
  5. Can you live with slow growth? Subscription income tends to start small and build. If you need a big payout next month, a launch-style product may suit you better.
  6. Does it match your brand? If you'd feel awkward telling your audience about it, they'll feel that too.
Test demand before you commit. Post a story poll like "Would you use a daily app from me?" or share a mock-up of what it could look like. Strong, specific replies are a much better signal than follower count.

A custom build gives you exactly what you want but costs the most. A DIY builder is cheaper, but you do the building, testing and App Store submission yourself. A platform app works well if you already sell a course or membership there. A white-label app is a finished, tested app branded for you, so you choose from what it already does rather than designing every feature. Good alternatives if an app isn't right

If the self-check came out mostly "no", that's useful too. You can still build income that doesn't depend on brand deals:

  • Digital products like templates, presets or guides, sold once from your bio link. See our digital products for influencers guide.
  • A paid community or newsletter for your most engaged followers.
  • Affiliate links for products you already recommend.

If you go ahead: tips for a good launch

  • Tell the story. Explain why you made it and who it's for. People support what they understand.
  • Show it in daily use. Your morning screen, your widget, the affirmation that helped you today. Real use is more convincing than a promo graphic.
  • Keep mentioning it. Most downloads come from steady, natural mentions over months, not from launch day.
  • Listen to early users. Their reviews and messages tell you what to highlight and how to describe it.
  • Be patient. Subscription income builds slowly. Judge it after a few months, not a few days.
