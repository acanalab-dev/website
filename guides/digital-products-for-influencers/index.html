Digital products for influencers: 10 ideas and how to sell them

Digital products let you earn from what your followers already ask you about, without stock or shipping. Here are 10 ideas for influencers, how they compare, and how to choose, price and sell your first one.

What counts as a digital product

A digital product is anything you make once and sell as a download, a login or an app, with no stock to store and nothing to ship. Each sale costs you almost nothing to deliver, which is why digital products are one of the most popular ways for influencers to earn beyond brand deals.

They work especially well for creators because your followers already trust your taste and know-how. If people keep asking how you edit your photos, plan your week or structure your workouts, you already have the start of a product. The job is to package that answer so people can buy it.

10 digital product ideas for influencers

Here are the most common types, roughly from quickest to make to biggest project.

  1. Presets and filters. Photo and video presets for Lightroom or editing apps. Ideal if your feed has a recognizable look and people ask how you get it.
  2. Templates. Canva templates for posts, Notion dashboards, budget spreadsheets, meal planners or media kits. Useful tools people can reuse every week.
  3. Printables. Habit trackers, journal pages, checklists, wall art and kids' activity sheets. Popular in parenting, home and wellness niches.
  4. Guides and e-books. A focused PDF that solves one problem, like "a 7-day reset for your morning routine" or "packing lists for a carry-on-only trip".
  5. Workout or meal plans. A four- or eight-week program with clear steps. Works well for fitness and nutrition creators, ideally with a note to check with a professional where it matters.
  6. Challenges. A paid 7-, 14- or 30-day challenge with daily prompts by email or in a group. The deadline and shared start date give people a reason to join now.
  7. Audio and video packs. Guided meditations, sleep stories, music loops, sound effects or stock footage from your own shoots.
  8. Mini-courses. A short set of lessons on one skill, like "edit Reels on your phone in 30 minutes". Smaller than a full course, so faster to make and easier to sell.
  9. Full online courses. A complete program on a valuable skill. The most work, with a higher price to match.
  10. Apps. A tool people use every day, like an affirmations, habit or workout app with your name on it. Usually sold as a monthly subscription rather than a one-time download.

Most of these are paid once. Apps, memberships and some challenges can be paid every month, which changes how your income grows. Our guide to revenue streams for creators explains how one-time and monthly income fit together.

How to choose your first digital product

The best first product is rarely the biggest idea. It's the smallest thing your audience has already asked for. Here's a quick way to find it:

  1. Read your own comments and DMs. Write down every question you get more than once. Repeated questions are product ideas.
  2. Check your most-saved posts. Saves mean "I want to come back to this". A post people save is often a product people would buy.
  3. Pick the format that fits the answer. A look becomes presets. A process becomes a template or guide. A habit becomes a challenge or an app.
  4. Ask before you build. Run a story poll or question box: "Would you want my weekly planner as a template?" A few dozen enthusiastic replies is a good sign.
  5. Make the smallest useful version. Ship something good in a week or two rather than something perfect in six months. You can improve it once people buy.
Name your product after the result, not the format. "Calm Sunday planning kit" sells better than "Notion template", because it says what the buyer gets.

How to price digital products

Pricing feels hard, but a few simple rules cover most cases:

  • Price by value, not effort. A template that saves someone two hours every week is worth more than the hour it took you to make.
  • Look at similar products. Search the marketplaces your followers use and see what comparable items cost. Aim for the middle, not the bottom.
  • Start a little higher than feels comfortable. It's easier to run a launch discount than to raise a price later.
  • Bundle when you have three. Once you have several related products, a bundle at a small discount often becomes your best seller.
  • Count the fees. Payment processors, marketplaces and app stores take a cut, and taxes may apply. Compare what you keep, not just the price.

Where to sell them

You have three main options, and many creators use more than one:

  • Link-in-bio stores. Creator storefront tools let you sell downloads straight from your profile link. Quick to set up and built for social traffic.
  • Marketplaces. Template and printable marketplaces bring their own buyers, which helps you get found by people who don't follow you yet, in exchange for a bigger fee and more competition.
  • Your own website. More control and a direct email list, but you bring all the traffic yourself.
  • App stores. For apps, the App Store handles payments, subscriptions and delivery. Your followers download it like any other app.

How to promote without feeling salesy

Most creators worry that selling will put their audience off. In practice, people are happy to buy something useful from someone they trust, as long as the content doesn't turn into one long ad.

  • Show it in use. Edit a photo with your preset, plan your week with your template, do day one of your challenge on camera.
  • Share the story. Why you made it, what problem it solved for you, and what early buyers said.
  • Mention it regularly, briefly. One clear mention a week works better than a launch week of nonstop posts followed by silence.
  • Keep giving free value. Your free content is what builds the trust that sells the product. Don't lock everything behind a paywall.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Building something nobody asked for. Validate with a poll or a waitlist first. A product that answers a real question sells itself more easily.
  • Overbuilding the first version. A 200-page e-book isn't better than a sharp 20-page guide. Shorter often sells better.
  • Launching once and stopping. Most sales come from steady, regular mentions over months, not from the launch post.
  • Making too many products at once. One product that sells well beats five that nobody knows about. Add the next once the first is working.
  • Skipping the basics. Use clear product pages, honest descriptions and a simple refund policy, and label sponsored or affiliate content in your posts.

Frequently asked questions

What digital products sell best for influencers?

Products that answer a question your followers already ask. Presets, templates, printables and short guides are the most common starting points, because they're quick to make and easy to understand.

How much should I charge for a digital product?

Price by the value to the buyer, not the hours it took you. Check what similar products cost on the marketplaces your followers use, aim for the middle, and remember to count platform and payment fees.

Do I need a big audience to sell digital products?

No. A small audience that trusts you can buy more readily than a large, passive one. Asking your followers what they want before you build makes the biggest difference.

Where should influencers sell digital products?

Most start with a link-in-bio store because it's quick and fits social traffic. Marketplaces help new buyers find you, your own website gives more control, and apps are sold through the app stores.

Can an app be a digital product?

Yes. Unlike most downloads, an app is used every day and is usually paid for monthly. With a white-label app, it's built and published for you, so it's realistic even without technical skills.
