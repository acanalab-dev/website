Do affirmations work? What the research says

Affirmations can work, but not in the "say it and it will happen" way. Research shows modest, real benefits, especially when affirmations connect to your values, and a clear way they can backfire. Here's what the studies say and how to use affirmations so they actually help.

The short answer

Yes, affirmations can work, but not in the magical "say it and it will happen" way they're sometimes sold. They work best as a tool for changing how you talk to yourself and how you react to pressure. They work worst when they're too far from what you actually believe.

The research is more interesting than either the hype or the eye-rolling. Studies show some real, modest benefits. They also show a clear way affirmations can backfire. Knowing both is what makes the difference between affirmations that help and ones that feel hollow.

What the research says

Most scientific work on this topic comes from self-affirmation theory, proposed by psychologist Claude Steele in 1988. The idea is that we all try to protect our sense of being a good, capable person. When something threatens that, like criticism, a setback or a stressful test, we tend to get defensive. Reminding ourselves of what we value and what we're good at can make that threat feel smaller, so we respond more calmly and openly.

A few findings stand out:

  • Values matter more than slogans. In many studies, the "affirmation" isn't a repeated phrase. People write briefly about values that matter to them, like family, kindness or creativity. A large 2014 review by Geoffrey Cohen and David Sherman found that these short exercises can reduce defensiveness and help people perform better under pressure.
  • Lower stress reactions. A 2005 study led by David Creswell found that people who reflected on their personal values before a stressful public-speaking task had a smaller stress-hormone response than people who didn't.
  • Changes in how the brain processes messages. Brain-imaging research, such as a 2016 study by Christopher Cascio and colleagues, found that self-affirmation activated areas linked to thinking about yourself and to reward. People were then more open to helpful health messages.

The effects are usually modest, and results vary between studies. Affirmations aren't a cure for anything. But there's reasonable evidence that they can shift your mindset in useful ways, especially when they connect to what you value.

When affirmations backfire

The most famous warning comes from a 2009 study by Joanne Wood and colleagues. They asked people to repeat "I'm a lovable person". People who already had high self-esteem felt slightly better. People with low self-esteem felt worse than if they hadn't said it at all.

The likely reason: when a statement clashes with what you believe about yourself, your mind argues back. "I'm lovable" gets answered with every memory that says otherwise, and you end up rehearsing the very doubts you wanted to quiet.

That doesn't mean people who struggle with self-worth should skip affirmations. It means the wording matters. A few signs an affirmation is working against you:

  • You feel a quick "that's not true" every time you say it.
  • It makes you compare yourself with how far you are from it.
  • It describes a result you can't control, like "Everyone will love my work".

How to make affirmations work for you

Based on the research and on what people find in practice, these changes make the biggest difference:

  1. Make it believable. Swap "I am confident" for "I'm learning to trust myself" or "I can feel nervous and still speak up". If the statement feels about 60% true, it's in the right zone.
  2. Connect it to your values. "I show up for the people I care about" is often stronger than "I am amazing", because it's rooted in something you already believe matters.
  3. Focus on actions, not outcomes. You control what you do, not how things turn out. "I prepare well" holds up better on a bad day than "I always succeed".
  4. Use them at the right moment. Affirmations help most right before something that tests you: a meeting, a hard conversation, a workout or a night when your thoughts race.
  5. Repeat them, but keep them fresh. A daily habit builds familiarity. Changing your affirmations every week or two stops them from becoming background noise.
  6. Pair them with a next step. "I can ask for help" works best when you then send the message.

10 believable affirmations to start with

These are deliberately gentle. They stretch you without asking you to believe something that feels false.

  1. I am learning to be kinder to myself.
  2. I can take one small step today.
  3. I don't have to be perfect to be worthy.
  4. I'm allowed to grow at my own pace.
  5. I can feel nervous and still show up.
  6. I've handled hard things before.
  7. Today, I'll notice what I did well.
  8. I'm open to seeing myself differently.
  9. My best is enough for today.
  10. I am becoming someone I'm proud of.

10 values-based affirmations

These follow the research most closely: they remind you of what matters to you, not of a result you're chasing.

  1. I care deeply about the people in my life.
  2. I keep my promises, especially to myself.
  3. I bring kindness to the people I meet.
  4. I value honesty, even when it's hard.
  5. I am curious and keep learning.
  6. I do my work with care.
  7. I make time for what matters most.
  8. My creativity is worth sharing.
  9. I stand up for what I believe in.
  10. I try to leave things a little better than I found them.
Want to try the values exercise from the studies? Pick the value that matters most to you from a list like the one above. Then spend 5 to 10 minutes writing about why it's important to you and a time it guided what you did.

A 2-week affirmation experiment

The best way to know whether affirmations work for you is to test them. Here's a simple two-week experiment.

  1. Day 1: Pick a focus. Choose one area, like confidence at work, calmer evenings or being kinder to yourself.
  2. Day 1: Choose two affirmations. One believable affirmation from the first list and one values-based one from the second, both related to your focus.
  3. Every day: Say them at a set time. Attach them to something you already do, like brushing your teeth or making coffee. Say each one slowly, three times.
  4. Every day: Rate it. Before bed, give yourself a score from 1 to 10 for how you handled your focus area today. It takes ten seconds.
  5. Day 14: Look back. Compare the first few scores with the last few. If you see a difference, keep going. If not, adjust the wording and try again.

What affirmations can't do

Being honest about the limits helps you get more out of them:

  • They don't replace action. Affirmations can make the next step feel possible. The step itself is what changes your situation.
  • They aren't a treatment. If you're dealing with depression, anxiety or other mental health concerns, affirmations can be a gentle extra, but they're not a substitute for support from a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. Our affirmations for depression are written with that in mind.
  • They won't change other people. They can change how you respond, which often matters more than it seems.

Frequently asked questions

Do affirmations actually work?

Research suggests they can have modest, real benefits, such as lower stress reactions and less defensiveness, especially when they connect to your personal values. They work best when they feel believable and are paired with action.

Is there scientific evidence for affirmations?

Yes. Studies based on self-affirmation theory, such as a 2014 review by Cohen and Sherman and a 2005 stress study by Creswell and colleagues, found benefits from reflecting on personal values. Effects are usually modest and vary between studies.

Why do affirmations make me feel worse?

If an affirmation feels untrue, your mind argues back and you end up focusing on your doubts. A 2009 study found this happened to people with low self-esteem. Softer, more believable wording usually fixes it.

How long does it take for affirmations to work?

Many people notice a difference in how they talk to themselves within two weeks of daily use. Try a simple two-week experiment and rate your day each evening to see if it helps you.

Can affirmations replace therapy?

No. Affirmations can be a helpful extra, but they aren't a treatment for depression, anxiety or other mental health conditions. If you're struggling, talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust.
